Kakuni (Braised Pork Belly) 角煮

▾

Surprise me with

Kakuni ("squared simmer") braises cubed pork belly in soy, sake, and mirin until meltingly tender, a dish with roots in Nagasaki's historical trade with China, closely related to Chinese red-braised pork.

Method

  1. Sear the pork belly cubes in a dry Dutch oven over medium-high heat for 5 to 8 minutes, turning, until golden on all sides, then pour off the fat.
  2. Cover the pork with water, add the ginger and green onion, bring to a boil and simmer gently, covered, for 1.5 to 2 hours, until a chopstick slides through easily. Pre-cooking in plain water renders the fat and makes the meat meltingly tender before any soy goes in; seasoning too early tightens it. Drain and rinse.
  3. Return the pork to the pot with the measured water, sake, mirin and sugar and simmer for 10 minutes so the sweetness soaks in first.
  4. Add the soy sauce and the eggs, lay a drop lid or a circle of parchment directly on the surface, and simmer over low heat for 30 to 40 minutes, turning once, until the liquid has reduced to a glossy glaze.
  5. Let it rest in the sauce for at least 30 minutes, or overnight; the flavor soaks in as it cools. Reheat gently and serve with the halved eggs, mustard and blanched greens.

Common mistakes

  • Rushing the initial plain simmer, which is what tenderizes the pork before it ever meets the flavoring liquid.
  • Adding the eggs too early, which can make them overcooked and rubbery by the time the dish finishes.

Authentic tips

  • The two-stage cooking (plain water first, then seasoned liquid) is what gives kakuni its signature melt-in-the-mouth texture without being overly salty.
  • This dish, like most braises, improves with an overnight rest in the refrigerator.

Substitutions

  • A pressure cooker cuts the braise to 45 minutes.
  • Pork shoulder gives a leaner dish.

Storage

Leftovers: Keeps 5 days refrigerated; the fat solidifies and can be lifted off.

Freezing: Freezes for 3 months in the sauce.
