Singapore Sweet and Sour Pork

▾

Surprise me with

Singapore's Cantonese-influenced version of sweet and sour pork leans slightly sweeter and often includes more fruit, reflecting the local preference for balanced, mild flavors across its multicultural food courts.

Method

  1. Mix the pork with the soy sauce, salt, pepper and egg and leave for 15 minutes. Toss each piece in cornstarch, pressing it on, and rest for 5 minutes so the coating hydrates.
  2. Heat the oil to 165 C (330 F) and fry the pork in batches for 5 to 6 minutes until set and lightly golden. Drain on a rack.
  3. Raise the oil to 190 C (375 F) and fry the pork again for 1 to 2 minutes until deep golden and crisp. The second, hotter fry is what keeps it crunchy once sauced.
  4. Mix the ketchup, plum sauce, vinegar, sugar, water and sauce cornstarch. Pour off all but 1 tablespoon of oil, fry the garlic and onion for 30 seconds, then the pepper, tomato and cucumber for 30 seconds.
  5. Pour in the sauce and bring it to a boil until thick and glossy, add the pineapple, then toss in the pork for 30 seconds until coated. Serve at once, before the crust softens.

Common mistakes

  • Tossing the pork in sauce too far ahead of serving, which softens the crispy coating.
  • Using canned pineapple syrup as a sauce base without adjusting sugar, which can make the dish overly sweet.

Authentic tips

  • Frying twice (rest, then re-fry briefly) gives an extra-crisp coating that holds up better once sauced.
  • Fresh pineapple gives a brighter, less syrupy flavor than canned, if available.

Substitutions

  • Chicken thigh works well with the same method for a lighter version.
  • No rice vinegar: white vinegar works, though flavor is slightly sharper.

Storage

Leftovers: Keeps 2 days refrigerated; the coating softens on reheating.

Freezing: Not recommended.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Steamed rice
  • Soup: Clear soup
  • Dessert: Fresh pineapple
  • Beverage: Chinese tea
