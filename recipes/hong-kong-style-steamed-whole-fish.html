Hong Kong-Style Steamed Whole Fish

▾

Surprise me with

Steamed whole fish is a centerpiece of Cantonese banquets and Sunday family dinners alike, prized because steaming preserves the fish's natural sweetness better than almost any other method. Serving a whole fish also carries a symbolic wish for abundance, since "yu" (fish) sounds like the word for "abundance" in Chinese.

Method

  1. Trim the belly fat and scrape out any dark blood along the spine inside the cavity, which tastes muddy. Rinse inside and out, pat dry, trim the fins and cut a long slit along the thick back on each side so the heat reaches the center.
  2. Lay the green onion greens on a heatproof plate as a rack for the fish, so steam circulates underneath. Put the fish on top and lay the ginger slices on and inside it. Mix the soy sauces, wine, water, sugar, salt and pepper into a sauce and warm it slightly.
  3. Set a rack in a flat-bottom wok or steamer, with the water just below the plate, and bring it to a full boil before the fish goes in. Steam covered over high heat for 10 to 12 minutes for a 700 g (1.5 lb) fish, a minute more or less for every 100 g. It is done when the eyes turn white and a chopstick slides easily into the thickest part, down to the bone.
  4. Lift the plate out, pour off the cloudy steaming liquid, which is fishy, and discard the cooked ginger and green onion.
  5. Pile the julienned ginger and shredded green onion whites on the fish. Heat the finishing oil until it shimmers and pour it over the aromatics so they sizzle, then pour the sauce around the fish. Finish with the sesame oil and cilantro and serve at once.

Common mistakes

  • Starting the fish in cold or lukewarm steamer water, which overcooks the outside before the inside is done.
  • Leaving the liquid that collects under the fish on the plate — it carries a strong, slightly bitter taste and should always be discarded.

Authentic tips

  • A fish that's truly fresh needs almost nothing else — resist the urge to add more sauce or spice than this recipe calls for.
  • Choose a fish no thicker than about 4 cm (1.5 in) at the thickest point so it cooks through evenly without drying out the tail end.

Substitutions

  • No whole fish available? Use two fish fillets (basa, tilapia) and reduce steaming time to 6–8 minutes.
  • Light soy sauce can be swapped for low-sodium soy sauce with no change to the method.

Storage

Leftovers: Flake any leftover fish and stir into the next day's congee or fried rice.

Freezing: Best eaten fresh; the texture suffers after freezing once cooked. Freeze the raw fish, not the finished dish, if needed.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Steamed rice
  • Soup: Choy sum

  • Beverage: Hot jasmine tea
