Ants Climbing a Tree 蚂蚁上树

Named for the way bits of ground pork cling to the glass noodles like ants on a branch, this playful Sichuan dish combines mung bean noodles with spicy doubanjiang-seasoned pork for a quick, vivid stir-fry.

  • Cook 10 min

Method

  1. Soak glass noodles in warm water 15 minutes until pliable; drain and cut into shorter lengths.
  2. Stir-fry ground pork in oil until browned and cooked through, breaking it up as it cooks.
  3. Add doubanjiang, garlic, and ginger, frying until fragrant and the oil turns red.
  4. Add soy sauce, wine, and stock, stirring to combine.
  5. Add the soaked glass noodles, tossing to coat and simmering 3–4 minutes until the noodles absorb most of the liquid.
  6. Garnish with green onion before serving.

Common mistakes

  • Not soaking the glass noodles long enough, which leaves tough, unevenly hydrated strands.
  • Using too little doubanjiang, which is the dish's main flavor driver — don't be shy with it.

Authentic tips

  • Cutting the soaked noodles into shorter lengths makes the dish much easier to eat and mix evenly.
  • This dish comes together very quickly once the noodles are soaked — have everything else prepped first.

Substitutions

  • Ground beef or chicken can replace pork with the same method.
  • No mung bean noodles: sweet potato glass noodles work as a substitute, with a slightly chewier texture.

Storage

Leftovers: Keeps 2 days refrigerated; noodles continue softening.

Freezing: Not recommended — glass noodle texture suffers.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Steamed rice
  • Soup: Hot and sour soup
  • Dessert: Fresh fruit
  • Beverage: Jasmine tea
