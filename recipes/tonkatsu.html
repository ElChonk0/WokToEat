Tonkatsu 豚カツ

▾

Surprise me with

Tonkatsu is Japan's breaded, deep-fried pork cutlet, adapted from Western cutlet dishes in the late 19th century and now a defining comfort food, served with shredded cabbage and tangy tonkatsu sauce.

Method

  1. Snip through the fat and connective tissue along the edge of each chop every 2 cm (3/4 in) so it does not curl, then pound lightly to an even thickness. Season with salt and pepper.
  2. Dredge in flour and shake off the excess, dip in the egg mixed with oil, then press firmly into the panko so it coats thickly. Rest for 5 minutes so the coating sets.
  3. Heat the oil in a Dutch oven to 170 C (340 F), about 5 cm (2 in) deep. Fry one or two chops at a time for 4 to 5 minutes, turning once, until golden and the pork is just cooked (63 C / 145 F).
  4. For an extra-crisp crust, raise the oil to 180 C (350 F) and fry each chop for 30 seconds more.
  5. Stand the chops upright on a rack for 2 minutes to drain and let the residual heat finish the center. Slice into strips and serve with finely shredded cabbage, rice, miso soup, tonkatsu sauce and mustard.

Common mistakes

  • Not scoring the fat edge of the chop, which can cause the meat to curl and cook unevenly.
  • Overcrowding the pan, which drops the oil temperature and produces a greasy crust.

Authentic tips

  • Panko's larger, airier crumbs give tonkatsu its distinctively light, crunchy crust compared to regular breadcrumbs.
  • Draining on a wire rack instead of paper towels keeps the bottom crust from steaming and going soft.

Substitutions

  • Chicken thigh, pounded flat, makes a good chicken katsu variation.
  • No tonkatsu sauce: a mix of ketchup, Worcestershire sauce, and a little soy sauce approximates it.

Storage

Leftovers: Keeps 2 days refrigerated; reheat in an oven or air fryer to restore crispness.

Freezing: Freezes well, breaded and uncooked, for up to a month.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Steamed rice and shredded cabbage
  • Soup: Miso soup
  • Dessert: Fresh fruit
  • Beverage: Hot sencha
