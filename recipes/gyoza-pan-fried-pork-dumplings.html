Gyoza (Pan-Fried Pork Dumplings) 餃子

▾

Surprise me with

Gyoza arrived in Japan from northern China after the Second World War, and Japanese cooks adapted them into garlic-scented, crisp-bottomed dumplings that became a ramen shop staple.

Method

  1. Toss the minced cabbage with the salt and leave for 15 minutes, then squeeze out as much water as you can. Wet cabbage makes soggy, splitting gyoza.
  2. Knead the pork with the garlic, ginger, sake, soy sauce, sesame oil and pepper by hand until sticky and pale, then mix in the cabbage, mushrooms and green onion.
  3. Put a level tablespoon of filling in the center of a wrapper, wet half the edge with water, fold it over and make 5 or 6 pleats along the front edge, pressing them against the flat back to seal.
  4. Heat half the frying oil in a large nonstick skillet over medium heat and arrange half the gyoza flat-side down in a ring. Fry for 2 to 3 minutes until the bottoms are golden.
  5. Pour in half the water, cover at once and steam for 3 minutes, until the water has almost gone and the wrappers are translucent.
  6. Uncover, drizzle half the finishing sesame oil around the edge and fry for 1 minute more until the bottoms are deep golden and crisp. Invert onto a plate and repeat. Serve with soy sauce, vinegar and chili oil mixed to taste.

Common mistakes

  • Too much filling makes sealing hard.
  • Skipping the squeeze makes the dumplings watery.

Authentic tips

  • Garlic chives are traditional in the filling.
  • Freeze uncooked on a tray and cook from frozen.

Substitutions

  • Ground chicken thigh replaces some pork.
  • Garlic chives replace cabbage.

Storage

Leftovers: Keeps 2 days refrigerated; re-fry in a nonstick pan.

Freezing: Freeze uncooked on a tray for 2 months; cook from frozen.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Steamed rice
  • Soup: Wakame soup
  • Dessert: Fresh fruit
  • Beverage: Cold beer
