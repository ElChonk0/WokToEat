Tonkotsu-Style Ramen 豚骨ラーメン

▾

Surprise me with

Tonkotsu ramen originated in Kurume and was popularized in Fukuoka, built on a broth of pork bones boiled for hours until they turn cloudy white and creamy — among the richest and most labor-intensive of Japan's regional ramen styles.

Method

  1. Blanch pork bones in boiling water 10 minutes; drain and rinse thoroughly, scrubbing off any residue.
  2. Combine bones, garlic, and ginger in a large pot with enough water to cover generously; bring to a boil, then boil hard, uncovered, 2.5–3 hours, topping up water as needed, until the broth turns milky white.
  3. Meanwhile, simmer pork belly in soy sauce, mirin, and a little water until tender, about 1 hour, then slice thin for chashu topping.
  4. Strain the broth, discarding solids, and season with a portion of the reserved chashu braising liquid to taste.
  5. Cook ramen noodles according to package directions; divide among bowls.
  6. Ladle hot broth over noodles, top with sliced chashu, soft-boiled egg, green onion, and nori.

Common mistakes

  • Simmering the broth too gently, which never achieves the emulsified, milky-white texture that defines tonkotsu — it needs a hard, rolling boil.
  • Skipping the initial blanch-and-scrub step, which leaves the broth with an unpleasant gamey off-flavor.

Authentic tips

  • A hard, rolling boil (not a gentle simmer) is what emulsifies the collagen and fat into the broth's signature creamy body.
  • This broth freezes and reheats exceptionally well; the long cook time makes a double batch worthwhile.

Substitutions

  • No trotters: neck bones alone still produce a good broth, just slightly less rich.
  • Store-bought chashu is a reasonable shortcut if time is limited.

Storage

Leftovers: The broth keeps 3 days refrigerated; store noodles separately.

Freezing: The broth freezes excellently for up to 3 months; cook fresh noodles when serving.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Gyoza
  • Soup: Not needed; the ramen is the meal
