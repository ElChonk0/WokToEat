Tonkotsu-Style Ramen 豚骨ラーメン

▾

Surprise me with

Tonkotsu ramen originated in Kurume and was popularized in Fukuoka, built on a broth of pork bones boiled for hours until they turn cloudy white and creamy — among the richest and most labor-intensive of Japan's regional ramen styles.

Method

  1. Soak the bones in cold water for 1 hour, then put them in a large pot of cold water, bring to a boil and boil hard for 10 minutes. Drain, scrub off the dark scum and blood and clean the pot. This first boil keeps the broth clean.
  2. Return the bones to the pot with the measured water, garlic, ginger and green onion. Bring to a rolling boil and keep it there, at a vigorous boil, for 10 to 12 hours, topping up with water as it reduces and stirring now and then. The hard boil is what emulsifies the collagen and marrow into the creamy, opaque white broth; a gentle simmer makes it clear.
  3. Meanwhile make the chashu: roll and tie the pork belly, brown it, then braise it with the soy sauce, mirin, sake, sugar and 240 ml (1 cup) water, covered, over low heat for 2 hours, turning occasionally. Cool and slice; use the liquid as tare and to marinate the eggs.
  4. Strain the broth; it should be milky and thick enough to coat a spoon. Season lightly with salt.
  5. Put 2 to 3 tablespoons of chashu tare in each bowl. Cook the noodles for 45 to 60 seconds so they stay firm (Hakata stalls serve them very firm), drain well and add to the bowls.
  6. Pour the boiling broth over and top with chashu, a halved egg, green onion, nori, wood ear and pickled ginger.

Common mistakes

  • Simmering the broth too gently, which never achieves the emulsified, milky-white texture that defines tonkotsu — it needs a hard, rolling boil.
  • Skipping the initial blanch-and-scrub step, which leaves the broth with an unpleasant gamey off-flavor.

Authentic tips

  • A hard, rolling boil (not a gentle simmer) is what emulsifies the collagen and fat into the broth's signature creamy body.
  • This broth freezes and reheats exceptionally well; the long cook time makes a double batch worthwhile.

Substitutions

  • No trotters: neck bones alone still produce a good broth, just slightly less rich.
  • Store-bought chashu is a reasonable shortcut if time is limited.

Storage

Leftovers: The broth keeps 3 days refrigerated; store noodles separately.

Freezing: The broth freezes excellently for up to 3 months; cook fresh noodles when serving.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Gyoza
  • Soup: Not needed; the ramen is the meal
  • Dessert: Fresh fruit
  • Beverage: Cold beer or barley tea
