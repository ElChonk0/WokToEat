Wonton Soup

▾

Surprise me with

Wonton soup has roots in northern Chinese cooking and traveled south and abroad with Cantonese immigrants, who refined the thin-skinned, delicately filled wonton now most associated with Hong Kong-style noodle shops.

Method

  1. Rinse the chopped pork, massage it briefly, then drain and squeeze it dry against a sieve. Toss the shrimp with their cornstarch and a pinch of pepper. A fatty cut chopped into small bits, not a paste, gives the juiciest, springiest filling.
  2. Mix the filling cornstarch, salt, sugar, pepper, oyster sauce and water into a smooth paste. Add the pork and ginger and stir for 30 to 45 seconds, then the sesame oil for 30 seconds. Lay the shrimp and green onion on top, cover and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes, or up to 2 days. Chilled filling is firmer and keeps the wrappers from turning soggy as you fold.
  3. Stir the filling briefly with a wet spoon. Put a teaspoon in the center of a wrapper, dab water on the edges, fold into a triangle and press out the air, then bring the two long corners together and pinch. Keep finished wontons on a floured tray under a cloth. Freeze any extras on the tray until firm, then bag them.
  4. Bring the stock to a simmer, season with the soy sauce and white pepper, and add the bok choy for 1 to 2 minutes until just tender. Keep the broth hot.
  5. Bring a separate large pot of water to a rolling boil and add the wontons, stirring gently so they do not stick. Once the boil returns, lower to medium and cook for 4 to 5 minutes in all, until they float and the filling is firm. Cooking them in plain water keeps the broth clear.
  6. Lift the wontons into bowls with a slotted spoon, add the bok choy and ladle the hot broth over.

Common mistakes

  • Overfilling the wrappers, which causes them to burst during boiling — a level teaspoon of filling per wonton is usually enough.
  • Boiling the wontons directly in the seasoned soup stock, which clouds the broth with starch — always cook them in plain water first, then add to the clear broth.

Authentic tips

  • This recipe makes more wontons than one meal needs on purpose — freeze the extra, uncooked, in a single layer before transferring to a bag, so they don't clump.
  • A small dish of chili oil or black vinegar on the side lets everyone adjust their own bowl.

Substitutions

  • All-pork or all-shrimp filling both work if you'd rather not mix proteins.
  • Store-bought wonton wrappers are standard even in professional kitchens — no need to make them from scratch.

Storage

Leftovers: Store cooked wontons and broth separately; combine when reheating to avoid soggy wrappers.

Freezing: Uncooked wontons freeze excellently for up to 2 months — drop straight from frozen into boiling water, adding a minute or two to the cooking time.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Not needed; the wonton are the dish
