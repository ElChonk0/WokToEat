Tantanmen (Japanese Spicy Sesame Ramen) 担々麺

Tantanmen is the Japanese adaptation of Sichuan dan dan noodles, introduced in the 1950s by the Sichuan-born chef Chen Kenmin, who added a rich sesame broth to suit Japanese tastes.

Method

  1. Heat the oil in a skillet over medium-high heat and fry the pork for 4 minutes until browned. Add the garlic, ginger and doubanjiang and fry for 1 minute, then add the sake, soy sauce and sugar and cook until dry and glossy. Set aside.
  2. Warm the stock in a pot over medium heat. Whisk the sesame paste, soy sauce and miso in a bowl with a ladle of the stock until smooth, then whisk it back into the pot with the soy milk.
  3. Heat the broth until steaming but not boiling, since soy milk splits if it boils hard. Keep it hot.
  4. Boil the noodles in plenty of water according to the packet, usually 1.5 to 2 minutes, adding the bok choy for the last minute. Drain well.
  5. Divide the noodles among warm bowls, ladle over the broth, top with the pork, bok choy and green onion, and finish with chili oil to taste.

Cook’s notes

Written independently, inspired by a dish featured on the Aaron and Claire YouTube channel.

Common mistakes

  • Boiling the soy milk broth makes it curdle; keep it just below a simmer.
  • Cooking the noodles in the broth makes it starchy and thick.

Authentic tips

  • Warm the bowls with hot water first so the soup stays hot.
  • A spoon of ground toasted sesame on top adds aroma.

Substitutions

  • Tahini plus 1 teaspoon sesame oil replaces neri goma.
  • Use regular milk or more stock if you have no soy milk.

Storage

Leftovers: Store the broth and pork separately for up to 3 days; cook noodles fresh.

Freezing: The pork topping freezes for 2 months; the broth is best made fresh.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Gyoza
  • Soup: The ramen is the soup
  • Dessert: Matcha ice cream
  • Beverage: Cold barley tea
