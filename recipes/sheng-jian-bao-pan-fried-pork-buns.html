Shēng Jiān Bāo (Pan-Fried Pork Buns) 生煎包

▾

Surprise me with

Sheng jian bao, pan-fried pork buns with a crisp base and juicy filling, are a Shanghai breakfast staple, sold at shops across the city.

Method

  1. Mix the flour, yeast, sugar and warm water and knead for 8 to 10 minutes into a smooth dough. Cover and leave for 30 to 45 minutes until about 1.5 times its size; sheng jian bao dough is only half-risen, so it stays chewy.
  2. Mix the pork with the ginger, soy sauce, Shaoxing wine, sugar, sesame oil and pepper until sticky, then fold in the chopped aspic and green onion. Chill. The aspic melts into soup inside the bun.
  3. Divide the dough into 20 pieces and roll each into a round about 8 cm (3 in) across, thinner at the edges. Fill with a heaping tablespoon of filling and pleat closed. Rest for 15 minutes.
  4. Heat the oil in a large nonstick skillet with a lid over medium heat. Arrange the buns pleat-side down (Shanghai style) or up, close together, and fry for 2 minutes until the bottoms start to color.
  5. Pour in the water, cover at once and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, until the water has evaporated and you hear sizzling.
  6. Uncover, scatter with sesame seeds and green onion, and fry for 1 to 2 minutes more until the bottoms are deep golden and crisp. Bite carefully; the soup is scalding.

Common mistakes

  • Too much water leaves the bases soggy; add only 100 ml.
  • Leaving the lid on too long after the water cooks off burns the bottoms.

Authentic tips

  • Pleats-up buns keep the soup inside.
  • Dip in black vinegar and a few strips of ginger.

Substitutions

  • Unflavored gelatin dissolved in hot stock replaces pork skin aspic.
  • Shrimp and pork make a classic variation.

Storage

Leftovers: Best fresh; leftovers keep 1 day and recrisp in a covered nonstick pan.

Freezing: Freeze uncooked buns for 2 months; fry from frozen with extra water and a longer steam.

Serve with

  • Side dish: Sweet soy milk or clear soup
  • Soup: Pickles
  • Dessert: Fresh fruit
  • Beverage: Hot green tea
