Part 2 — Cooking Education

These techniques recur across nearly every recipe in this collection. Learn them once here, and each recipe will simply point back to this section rather than re-explaining.

2.1 Knife Skills

The bias cut — slicing diagonally across vegetables like carrot, celery, and green onion — increases surface area for faster, more even cooking in a hot wok and a more attractive finished plate. Julienne (thin matchsticks) suits carrot and ginger; batonnet is a slightly thicker version of the same cut.

To mince ginger or garlic quickly, smash it first with the flat of the knife to break the fibers, then mince — faster and more aromatic than mincing from whole. For meats like chicken thigh or flank steak, slicing thinly against the grain shortens the muscle fibers and makes the finished dish — bulgogi, yakitori, stir-fried beef — noticeably easier to chew.

2.2 Stir-Frying

Mise en place is not optional in stir-frying. Once the wok is hot, there is no time to chop — every ingredient should be cut, every sauce pre-mixed in a small bowl, before the burner goes on.

Standard order: oil and aromatics (garlic, ginger, scallion whites) first over high heat, for seconds rather than minutes, so they perfume the oil without burning; protein next, cooked most of the way through and removed if the dish needs multiple stages; vegetables in order of cooking time, longest first; sauce added last and tossed quickly to coat and reduce slightly.

“Wok hei” — literally “breath of the wok” — is the smoky, faintly charred flavor that comes from very high heat and quick tossing. It's hard to fully replicate on a home stove, but a well-preheated wok or cast-iron pan, never overcrowded, and worked in batches if necessary, gets most of the way there. See 2.11 for electric and induction stoves.

2.3 Braising & Claypot Cooking

Red braising (hong shao) browns the protein first, then simmers it slowly in soy sauce, sugar, wine, and aromatics until the sauce reduces to a glossy coating. Low heat and time — not high heat — build the flavor here.

Claypot rice cooks raw or partially soaked rice directly in a seasoned clay pot with marinated protein layered on top. The reward is the crisp, slightly charred rice crust (faan jiu) that forms against the hot clay walls — achieved by raising the heat briefly near the end of cooking rather than throughout.

A cast-iron Dutch oven is the reliable substitute for a clay pot on any stove, and every recipe in this book that calls for a clay pot works in one. Clay pots can crack on electric coils and do not work on induction unless they are induction-rated or sit on a diffuser plate.

2.4 Steaming

A reliable rule for whole or filleted fish: roughly 10 minutes of steaming per inch of thickness at the thickest point, checked by whether the flesh flakes easily and turns opaque.

For egg custards — chawanmushi, steamed egg — keep the steam gentle, on medium-low heat with the lid slightly vented. A hard boil produces a pitted, rubbery texture instead of a silky one.

Bamboo steamers let excess moisture escape, avoiding the sogginess that can happen with metal steamers, which trap condensation. Line a bamboo steamer with cabbage leaves or parchment to prevent sticking.

2.5 Deep Frying

Oil temperature guide: roughly 325–350°F (165–175°C) for a slower, gentler cook, suited to delicate items or the first fry of a double-fry; 350–375°F (175–190°C) for a faster, crisper finish.

Double-frying — Korean fried chicken, char siu skin, karaage — fries once at a lower temperature to cook through, rests, then fries again at a higher temperature for shattering crispness. The first fry drives out moisture; the second fry crisps a now-drier exterior.

Always dry food thoroughly before frying, and never overcrowd the pot — both moisture and overcrowding drop the oil's temperature and produce soggy, greasy results instead of crisp ones.

2.6 Velveting Meat

A Cantonese technique: thin slices of meat — chicken, beef — are marinated briefly in egg white or cornstarch, sometimes with a small amount of baking soda for tougher cuts, then either blanched briefly in warm oil or water, or cooked directly in the stir-fry.

The science: the starch and egg-white coating seals in moisture during the brief high-heat cook, while baking soda raises the surface pH just enough to keep the meat tender rather than tough. This is the difference between home stir-fried beef and the noticeably more tender version served in restaurants.

2.7 Making Stock

A basic chicken stock: chicken bones (or a whole carcass), water just covering, simmered gently — never boiled hard, which clouds the stock — for 2 to 4 hours with ginger and scallion, skimming foam in the first 20 minutes.

Quick dashi: steep kombu in just-below-boiling water for 10 minutes, removing it before the water boils (boiling kombu turns the stock bitter and slimy), then add bonito flakes and steep off-heat for a few minutes before straining.

Freeze stock flat in zip-top bags, or in ice cube trays for small, fast-thawing portions — useful for weeknight soups that need just a cup or two.

2.8 Cooking Perfect Rice

Rinse rice in a bowl or strainer, swirling and draining, until the water runs mostly clear — this removes the surface starch that would otherwise make the rice gummy.

The classic “knuckle method” for water level: level the rinsed rice in the pot, then add water until it reaches the first knuckle line on a finger touching the rice's surface. It works regardless of pot size, and is the method most home cooks across East and Southeast Asia actually use instead of measuring.

Let rice rest, covered, off the heat for 10 minutes after cooking, before fluffing — this evens out the moisture and texture throughout the pot.

2.9 Noodle Preparation

Fresh noodles — hor fun, ramen — usually need only a brief blanch. Shock them immediately in cold water if they'll be stir-fried afterward; this stops the cooking and rinses off excess starch that would otherwise cause clumping.

For meal prep, slightly undercook noodles destined for a later stir-fry or soup, since they'll finish cooking when reheated — this is what prevents mushy leftovers on day two or three.

2.10 Balancing Flavor

Most dishes in this collection balance five elements: sweet, salty, sour, spicy, and umami. Regional cuisines lean differently — Thai cooking often foregrounds sour and spicy in tension with each other; Cantonese cooking favors umami and a light touch of sweetness with a restrained hand on salt; Korean cooking leans on fermented savoriness (doenjang, gochujang) balanced by sweetness.

Taste as you cook, and adjust at the end rather than only at the start. A dish that tastes flat almost always needs a small hit of acid — vinegar, lime — or a small hit of salt — soy, fish sauce — rather than more of everything at once.

2.11 Electric & Induction Stoves

These recipes are written to work on gas, electric and induction. The main differences are how fast the pan heats and how fast it cools.

Woks. Use a flat-bottom wok, 14 inches (35 cm) if you can, in carbon steel or cast iron. On induction the base must touch the glass, so a round-bottom wok will not heat; a flat-bottom wok also sits properly on an electric coil. Preheat for 3 to 5 minutes on the highest setting until a drop of water vanishes at once, then add the oil. Electric and induction burners lose heat when cold food hits the pan and do not recover as fast as gas, so cook in smaller batches (half a pound or 250 g of protein at a time), pat ingredients dry, and use the times in the recipes as a guide to doneness, not a stopwatch. Real wok hei needs a gas flame; a very hot flat-bottom carbon-steel wok and small batches get close.

Clay pots. Replace with a cast-iron enameled Dutch oven, or any heavy lidded pot. Heat it over medium and let it come up slowly, since a thick pot holds heat for a long time after you turn the burner down. For crisp rice crust (claypot rice), raise the heat for the last 2 minutes and then rest the pot off the heat.

Heat control. Gas answers instantly; electric coils and many ceramic tops take a minute to respond. When a recipe says to reduce the heat to low, move the pan off the burner for 30 seconds first, or switch to a smaller burner. Induction responds almost as fast as gas, so it suits caramel, simmering and shallow frying well. Use a pan that matches the burner size.

Open-flame steps. Charring eggplant, onion and ginger, or softening banana leaves, is written to work under a broiler, in a dry cast-iron skillet, or in boiling water. A gas flame or charcoal gives more smoke, so the recipe notes that where it matters.

Pan checks for induction. A magnet should stick firmly to the base. Cast iron, carbon steel and most stainless pans pass; aluminum, copper, glass and ceramic do not unless they have a magnetic base.
