Part 1 — Pantry & Ingredient Guide

Before any recipe in this collection, it helps to know the ingredients that define these cuisines. Stock the items below and most recipes become a matter of technique rather than shopping. Each entry explains what the ingredient does, when to reach for it, and what to use if you can't find it.

1.1 Soy Sauce Varieties

Soy sauce is not one ingredient but a family of them, and Cantonese and Chinese cooking in particular distinguish carefully between them.

  • Light (or “superior”) soy sauce — thinner, saltier, golden-brown. The everyday seasoning and dipping sauce; the one to keep on the table.
  • Dark soy sauce — aged longer, often with added molasses; less salty but thicker and far more deeply colored. Used in small amounts to color braises (char siu, lu rou fan, red-braised dishes) without oversalting them.
  • Double dark / “old” soy sauce — very thick, almost syrupy. Used by the teaspoon purely for color, rarely for seasoning.
  • Mushroom soy sauce — light soy infused with straw mushroom for extra umami depth. A good vegetarian stand-in for oyster sauce.
  • Tamari — a naturally gluten-free alternative; flavor differs slightly but works in any recipe in this collection.
  • Low-sodium soy sauce — roughly 40% less sodium than regular. This collection defaults to it where possible — taste and adjust, since brands vary widely in saltiness.

Rule of thumb: light soy for seasoning, dark soy for color. Most stir-fries use mostly light soy with a teaspoon of dark stirred in at the end.

1.2 Oyster Sauce

A thick, glossy, savory-sweet sauce made from oyster extractives, sugar, and cornstarch. It defines the flavor of classic Cantonese stir-fries — beef and broccoli, gai lan with oyster sauce — and is salty and intensely flavored, so a little goes a long way. Vegetarian “mushroom-flavored” versions made from shiitake substitute well in any recipe here.

1.3 Fish Sauce

Fermented anchovy liquid, the backbone of Thai, Vietnamese, and Filipino cooking the way soy sauce anchors Chinese cooking. It's used both in cooking — a splash in a soup or stir-fry — and raw, diluted, as the base of dipping sauces like nuớc chấm. Quality varies hugely: a good fish sauce (anchovy and salt only, ideally a higher %N nitrogen listed on the label) tastes savory rather than purely fishy. Start with less than the recipe seems to want; you can always add more.

1.4 Sesame Oil

Toasted (dark) sesame oil is nutty and fragrant, used as a finishing oil — a few drops stirred in right at the end of cooking. It is never the main cooking oil: it has a low smoke point and turns bitter and acrid at high heat. Refrigerate after opening, since it turns rancid faster than neutral oils.

1.5 Shaoxing Wine

An amber Chinese rice wine, slightly nutty and savory. It shows up constantly in marinades (especially alongside cornstarch and soy for velveting, see Part 2) and in stir-fries, where a splash is added to a hot pan and allowed to sizzle off for a second before the next ingredient goes in. Dry sherry is the closest substitute; for an alcohol-free version, use rice vinegar diluted with a little water and a pinch of sugar — not identical, but workable.

1.6 Mirin

A sweet Japanese rice wine used in glazes and simmered dishes — teriyaki, oyakodon, nikujaga. “Hon mirin” (true mirin) gets its sweetness from fermentation and carries a small amount of alcohol; “aji-mirin” (mirin-style seasoning) is cheaper, lower-alcohol, and has added sugar and salt — fine for cooking, but check the label, since it changes how much extra sugar or salt a recipe actually needs. No mirin on hand? Use sake (or dry sherry) with a little added sugar.

1.7 Rice Vinegar

Mild, clean acidity — far gentler than Western white vinegar. Used in sushi rice, quick pickles, and dipping sauces. “Seasoned” rice vinegar already has sugar and salt added; plain rice vinegar lets you control that yourself, which suits this book's lower-sodium approach better.

1.8 Black Vinegar

Chinkiang (Zhenjiang) vinegar is dark, malty, and faintly smoky — closer to a mild balsamic than to rice vinegar. Essential in dumpling dipping sauces, hot and sour soup, and some Shanghainese braises. Balsamic vinegar, while not a true substitute, is the closest pantry stand-in in a pinch.

1.9 Gochujang

Korean fermented chili paste — sweet, salty, savory, and spicy all at once, made from chili powder, glutinous rice, and fermented soybeans. It's the base of bibimbap sauce, dakgalbi marinade, and tteokbokki, and keeps for months refrigerated.

1.10 Gochugaru

Korean red chili flakes — coarser and fruitier than generic chili flakes, with moderate, rounded heat. Essential for kimchi and many Korean soups and stews. Standard chili flakes are hotter and more one-dimensional; not a true substitute, but workable at about half the quantity if gochugaru is unavailable.

1.11 Doubanjiang

Sichuan fermented broad bean and chili paste — salty, funky, deeply savory. The defining ingredient of mapo tofu and dan dan noodles. Pixian doubanjiang, named for the county where it originates, is considered the standard-bearer and worth seeking out by name.

1.12 Thai Curry Pastes

  • Red curry paste — made from dried red chilies; the hottest and most commonly used.
  • Green curry paste — made from fresh green chilies; herbal, bright, often hotter than it looks.
  • Yellow curry paste — milder and turmeric-forward, closer in spirit to South Asian curry.

Store-bought pastes (Mae Ploy and Maesri are widely available and reliable) are excellent and what most home cooks — and plenty of professional kitchens — actually use. These three pastes are a useful lens on regional identity: alongside gochujang and doubanjiang, each defines its cuisine's “spicy backbone” in a completely different direction — fermented-sweet, fermented-funky, fresh-herbal.

1.13 Coconut Milk

Canned, full-fat coconut milk separates into a thick cream (top) and thinner milk (bottom) when chilled or left to stand; many Thai curry recipes call for frying that cream separately first to render its oil before adding curry paste. “Light” coconut milk is simply diluted — don't substitute it 1:1 without expecting a thinner curry.

1.14 Miso

Fermented soybean paste. White (shiro) miso is mild, sweet, and fast-fermented; red (aka) miso is aged longer, bolder, and saltier; awase is a pre-blended mix of both. White miso is the most versatile starting point for a home pantry and keeps for months refrigerated.

1.15 Dashi

Japanese stock, most often made from kombu (dried kelp) and katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes). Instant dashi powder (hondashi) makes a perfectly respectable weeknight stock in under a minute and is what most Japanese households actually reach for day to day. A vegetarian version uses kombu and dried shiitake instead of bonito.

1.16 Noodles, by Type

  • Wheat noodles — ramen (alkaline, springy), udon (thick, chewy), somen (thin, delicate).
  • Egg noodles — thin Hong Kong–style (wonton noodles, pan-fried noodles) versus thicker Shanghai-style.
  • Rice noodles — dried vermicelli (bún), fresh flat rice noodles (hor fun, used in pad see ew), and banh phở.
  • Glass / cellophane noodles — made from mung bean starch; used in japchae and fresh spring rolls, turning translucent and chewy when cooked.

1.17 Rice

  • Jasmine rice — long-grain and fragrant; the default for most Southeast Asian and Cantonese meals in this collection.
  • Short-grain Japanese / Korean rice — stickier; used for sushi rice, bibimbap, and onigiri.
  • Glutinous (sticky) rice — used for claypot rice, lo mai gai, and desserts.

Rinse rice until the water runs mostly clear to remove surface starch and prevent gumminess. As a starting ratio, jasmine rice takes roughly 1 cup water per cup of rice in a rice cooker (a touch less on the stovetop, where less water evaporates); short-grain rice usually takes slightly more. See Part 2 for the full technique.

1.18 Oils

  • Neutral, high smoke point — vegetable, peanut, canola, or avocado oil, for stir-frying and deep frying.
  • Flavor oils — toasted sesame oil, chili oil — added at the end, never used for cooking.

This collection favors peanut, avocado, or canola oil over lard or heavy-handed pours of oil generally, without giving up the high heat that good stir-fry technique depends on — a heart-healthier choice that doesn't cost flavor.

1.19 Spice Guide

  • Five-spice powder — star anise, cloves, cinnamon, Sichuan pepper, and fennel; used in marinades and braises such as char siu.
  • White pepper vs. black pepper — Cantonese cooking favors white pepper for a cleaner, more floral heat in soups and marinades; black pepper shows up more in dishes with Singaporean, Malaysian, or Western influence (black pepper beef, black pepper crab).
  • Star anise — warm and licorice-like; a backbone of red braises and pho broth.
  • Sichuan peppercorns — not a true pepper — citrusy and numbing (the “ma” in málà). Toast briefly before grinding for the best flavor.
  • Dried chilies — used whole in Sichuan and Hunan cooking for heat and aroma, often without eating the chili itself.

1.20 Substitution & Lower-Sodium Chart

A quick reference for the ingredients above that carry the most sodium, and how to soften their impact without losing the dish.

Ingredient Typical Use Lower-Sodium Option Notes
Light soy sauce Everyday seasoning Low-sodium soy sauce or tamari Taste before adding salt elsewhere in the dish
Oyster sauce Stir-fry sauces Vegetarian mushroom-flavored oyster sauce Naturally lower-sodium versions exist
Fish sauce Soups, dipping sauces Use less; balance with lime juice or sugar Hard to replace entirely — dilute rather than omit
Doubanjiang Sichuan dishes Use slightly less; round out saltiness with a splash of soy No reliable low-sodium version exists
Table salt (general) All dishes Finish with acid (vinegar, lime) instead of more salt A small amount of MSG can replace a larger amount of salt for the same perceived seasoning
Canned coconut milk Curries Light coconut milk + 1 tsp coconut oil Restores some richness lost to dilution

Nutrition figures are per-serving estimates, not lab values. Side rice and noodles are excluded unless the dish contains them. See the guides for how they were calculated.
